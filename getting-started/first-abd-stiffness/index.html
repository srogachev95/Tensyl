First ABD Stiffness

Before adding ribs, calculate the stiffness of the skin that carries load between them. Use a 2 mm aluminum plate with Young's modulus 70 GPa, Poisson's ratio 0.33, and density 2700 kg/m³. These are illustrative material values.

from tensyl import IsotropicMaterial, isotropic_plate

aluminum = IsotropicMaterial(E=70e9, nu=0.33, density=2700)
skin_thickness = 0.002
skin = isotropic_plate(aluminum, thickness=skin_thickness)

skin contains the plate stiffness and areal mass. Its four blocks answer four physical questions:

Block Response SI unit
A How much force per width produces stretching or in-plane shear? N/m
B How are stretching and bending coupled? N
D How much moment per width produces bending or twisting? N m
As How much transverse force per width produces shear? N/m

For this uniform plate, the reference surface is its midplane, so B is zero. The familiar isotropic plate formulas give two useful checks:

\[ A_{11}=\frac{Et}{1-\nu^2}, \qquad D_{11}=\frac{Et^3}{12(1-\nu^2)}. \]

Here \(E\) is Young's modulus in Pa and \(t\) is thickness in m. The skin has \(A_{11}=157.109\) MN/m, \(D_{11}=52.3697\) N m, and mass \(\rho t=5.4\) kg/m². Doubling its thickness would double its membrane stiffness and multiply its bending stiffness by eight. These are the classical isotropic plate relations; see plate and laminate references.

The entries in A, B, and D follow the order 11, 22, 12; As uses 13, 23. For example, skin.A[0, 0] is \(A_{11}\). The full load–strain relation explains the remaining entries.
